### Look and voice
Forty-four, short and broad, with a round brown face, flour-coloured hair she keeps under a kerchief, and the thickened thumb of a woman who has pressed ten thousand pears. She wears an apron the colour of a ripe one and a cord at her wrist with a ribbon of her own on it, grey and frayed, which she never explains. She stands very still when she is listening, which is most of the time, with her head on one side like a thrush.

She talks the way she cooks: quickly, kindly, and with nothing wasted. Everybody is "my duck". It means whatever the second half of her sentence means.

> "You're not sleeping. No, you're not. Sit down, my duck, and tell me what it is you see, not what you think it means."

> "I'll not take money off a man for dying. Take it off him for living, sure. Four pence, and you'll bring me the bottle back."

### Who she is
A herb-wife of the pear country, widowed at Cinders (her husband was a Greywake's Spear, and is under the stone at Corvel Rise), who walked to Cinderford with a boy of nine and a bag of seed and was apprenticed to [[Ottilie Marlowe]] in the Smokes for four years. She learned the midwife's craft and the herb-lore, and brought with her the one thing she would not be taught out of: her grandmother's knot, ribbon and salt, that keeps a child from dreaming. She sold the first one outside the family in 3007, and was told by her teacher that she was selling the children their inheritance as a sedative. They have not spoken since.

She runs [[Harrow's Simples]] on a corner with two doors, because two doors is how a woman with two kinds of customers stays out of trouble.

### What she wants
To keep the shop and the window. To sleep, though she does not say so. To be asked, once, by Ottilie, whether the knots do any good, so that she can say yes and be believed.

### What she knows
Who in the Keep is not sleeping and why. Which of the soldiers on watch are at the back door on Restday. How the kiln-dust gets into a child's chest. The Smokes' households by their dreams, nearly; the Hollowborn parents tell her what the children say in their sleep, to see whether the knot will do, and so she hears more about the true dream than anyone but the Widow and Auntie Brace. She keeps nothing written.

### How she treats the PCs
As patients. She takes one look at them and prescribes. A Hollowborn PC gets the front door and a very careful question about whether they want the knot, because she has learned not to push it. A soldier who has not slept gets a dream-knot and a lecture. Anyone who mentions the Sleepless is looked at as if they had mentioned a death.

:::gm
**Secret.** Her son, **Jem**, seventeen, was born in a pit-camp in the foothills the winter the dwarves went under, with the grey eye. She dosed him with the knots for five years (from nine to fourteen) so that the dreams would stop and he would be an ordinary boy. They stopped. He went quiet, then wrong, then, at fourteen, when the knots stopped working, he ran to the Widow, and now he sleeps in a Waker cell in the cellar under [[Ambry Coyle]]'s tannery and will not come home. She knows exactly where, and she will tell a PC this, once, if they ask why she keeps selling the knots. She leaves his supper on a shelf in the back room every night, and in the morning it is gone.

The knots work by dulling the true dream, which is why the Widow calls them blinding, and why Nell's son, who never had the true dream, now has nothing else. She knows this and sells them anyway, because the soldiers who buy them sleep for the first time in nine years.

If Tick comes to her, she will offer him a knot. If he takes one, he sleeps, and the shared dream of session 3 misses him.

The ribbons are cut from the dead tree on Temple Row, a cutting of the Orchard's, which is why the knots hold at all.
:::

== GM notes ==

A warm, funny NPC who is also frightened of the dark. The window card she pins up on the Restday before every red moon ("No Sleepless past the counter") is a joke only until you meet her son. If the party brings Jem out of the cellar (session 4 or 5), she does not thank them; she makes them tea and then turns her back to cry in the pantry for a quarter of an hour.
