### Look and voice
A short, broad, quiet man of forty-five with cutter's forearms, a face the glass-dust has made older, and hands that are a map of fine white scars from wrist to knuckle. He wears a leather apron with glass plates sewn into the front, the way the Glassers do, and his hair cropped to nothing because long hair catches the dust. There is a pick in his hand or within a foot of it at all times. He is polite to everyone, in a soft careful voice, and he never raises it, and after a minute in his company you understand that the politeness is not a kindness, it is a wall he keeps between the world and what is on his cutting-floor.

> "You're welcome to the water. Mind the square. Don't touch anybody. That's all the law we have, and it's enough."

> "Everyone asks why I cut here. Where else would I cut? She's here."

> "A Glasser came for her with a pick, once. He wanted the necklace off the smith's wife and he thought he'd start with mine for practice. I'm not a violent man. I didn't have to be twice."

### Who he is
Jeb Marrowick was a cider-man's son and a kiln-hand, and he married Aud Teal, of the pear-country Teals, old Gammer Teal's grand-niece, the spring before the war ended, and on the night of Cinders he was in the kiln cellar banking the fire while she stood at the kiln's mouth, six months gone, calling down to him to come up and look at the sky. He came up in the morning. She was still there. She is still there, by the kiln, one hand on the small of her back and the other raised toward the east, and the child with her, and in nine years he has not moved his cutting-floor further than a man's reach from her feet.

He is headman because the eleven who came up out of the cellars needed one and he was the one who did not leave. He cuts slag for the Cinderford kilns, which is the village's whole trade with the world, and he has taught the Pellows to cut, and he keeps the Old Post swept and the goats counted and the square untouched. In 3009 a Fort Mercy crew came for the smith's wife's necklace, and he killed the crew-boss at the first stroke, and the square turned, and Vexa Glassjaw herself came a month later and the two of them talked for an hour by the kiln without once looking at each other, and since then Ambercote is the one place in the Ashenreach the Glassers do not dig. He does not call it a truce. He calls it manners.

### What he wants
Salt and iron, which Kettle's cousins bring. To be left alone with his dead, which the world mostly allows. For nobody with a pick to come within a day of the square, ever. He wants nothing from the company and will say so, courteously: "You hold a ford. We're not on it."

Underneath, and he does not say this: he wants to know whether she is in there.

### What he knows
The southern glass better than any Glasser, because he walks it for slag and they do not. Where the glass thins and where it runs deep, where the shapes in it are thickest, and a way across it to the Rent's southern lip that no chart shows, which he has walked once and will not walk again. That the two men in Salt Guard tabards in the square were never villagers and arrived that night running. That Kettle's cousins ask, every visit, very casually, whether anyone from the Smokes has been by; he does not know why and has decided not to. That at red moon the standing turn east, every one, and that his niece hears them. That Vexa Glassjaw is afraid of Ambercote in a way that has nothing to do with him.

### How he treats the PCs
With courtesy and no warmth. Soldiers of the Ninth are "the ford's people" and are welcome to water, cheese at the price, and a night in the cider-house cellar, and are watched. Anyone carrying a pick, a mattock or a sapper's tools is asked, once, pleasantly, to leave them outside the village, and if they do not, the conversation is over. A Hollowborn is looked at longer than is comfortable and then treated with great gentleness, because of Cass. Anyone who stands beside Aud and does not touch her and does not say anything is, without a word on either side, allowed to stay as long as they like.

### The secret
He has heard her. At the full Ember of 16 Ashfall 3010, a year before the campaign, sitting on his cutting-floor at midnight with his back against her feet as he does every red moon, he heard a voice at his shoulder that was hers, say one word: "Cold." He is not Hollowborn and should not have heard anything, and he has told nobody, not Mardy, not Cass, and he has sat there every red moon since and heard nothing more. It is why he has never asked Cass what the standing say to her. He is afraid it is the same word.

(It is not. The standing say "not yet", with the Ninth's voice behind theirs. What Jeb heard was the Sleeper, which can counterfeit a dead wife as easily as a god, and which wants him to want her back, because a man who wants his wife out of the glass is a man who might, one day, let the glass break. If the Sleeper clock reaches 6, he hears her again, and she says "Let me out.")

### What changes
Come without picks, buy cheese, stand quietly by Aud, and Jeb will trade: the goat-track to Tally's Cut, water, a bed, the roll, and in session 6, if asked, the way across the southern glass toward the Rent that no chart shows (a day shorter than the Ash Road, and worse). Tell him what the Sleepless crew means to do to the two Salt Guard men and he will thank you, politely, and sharpen the pick, and the only way to stop a killing in the square is to stop the crew before it gets there. If a PC cuts, chips or so much as taps a standing figure in his sight, he attacks, and does not stop, and the square turns; the party will have to kill him or leave Ambercote at a run, and either way Cass is in the lane behind them, watching.

If the ward is resealed, Aud slumps an inch with the rest, and sets down her hand, and Jeb sits on his cutting-floor and does not cut for a day, and then cuts. If it cracks, she walks east, and he walks beside her.

== GM notes ==

**Voice:** soft, short, courteous; never raises it; a pick always within reach. His joke is that Ambercote's whole law is "don't touch anybody", said as if it were a great deal of law. His tragedy is on his cutting-floor and he has made his peace with it, which is the most frightening thing about him.
