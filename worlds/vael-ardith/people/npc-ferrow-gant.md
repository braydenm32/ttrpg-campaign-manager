### Look and voice
A tall man of fifty folded down to the height of a shorter one, with a chest that moves on one side only and a stillness about him that is not calm but economy. Grey beard cut square, grey eyes, grey everything; the glass-dust is in him to the bone. He wears a Regency quartermaster's sash under his coat where the diggers cannot see it and a brass thimble on a cord round his neck, and he carries a cutter's pick he has not swung in anger for three years, "because the last time it took me a week to breathe". He moves slowly, speaks slowly, and pours as if the thimble were a sacrament.

The voice is low, unhurried and kind, with a digger's humour that comes out flat and lands late.

> "We don't call it grave-robbing, friend. The graves came to us. We were just standing here."

> "Five silver a day for a cutter. Yes. That's what a man's worth who'll go down a hole in the dark and bring you up what's in it. Your captain pays you five copper to stand on a wall and look at the dark from the top. Think about that on your way home."

> "I was holding the lamp. The pick went through and the air came out, six years old, and it smelt of cider and candle-wax and ink. I knew him before I saw him. He always smelt of ink. He said it was the only thing about the job that didn't wash off."

### Who he is
Ferrow Gant was a corporal of the Regency's 11th Foot, a line regiment, and the regiment's quartermaster-sergeant for twenty years was a sour, exact, universally loved man the 11th called Tally because he counted everything twice. On the night of Cinders the 11th was on the plain east of the pear country when the sky came down; the regiment's baggage was at a farmstead called Lowbarrow, with the pay-chest and Tally counting it, and Ferrow was two miles off with a pike and a cough he had caught at Ardmere, and he lived because of the two miles. Six hundred of the 11th did not. He walked west in the morning with one working lung, found no regiment to report to and no way to say so at home, and in Seedwake 3003 found Vexa Glassjaw hiring strong backs at the Low Quay, and took it, because digging was the only trade the ash offered a man who could not march.

On 3 Ashfall 3008 he was holding the lamp at Lowbarrow when the pick went through, and the man inside the glass bubble, grey and whole and still sitting at his count, was Tally. Ferrow named him to the crew, and the crew named the pit, and he has kept the grave since. He has been crew-boss of the Cut since the first windlass went up, and has kept the Ripe One since the first tarpaulin, and the Cut trusts his thimble and his splits because he has never once lied about either. He has one lung and a chest that whistles in a greywind, and he will not die of it this year, probably.

### What he wants
A small thing that is not small. In the chest with the silver was a roll of the 11th's company seals, the quartermaster's own: the paper of a dead regiment, claims and vaults and widows' pensions, each seal a lead disc on a ribbon with the regiment's number and a clerk's mark. Vexa has the roll in her records vault at Fort Mercy and has never seen the use of it. Ferrow wants one seal, the pensions seal, carried to "a widow of the 11th" at Whitepost: Bethan, who keeps the back room at [[The Sunburst]] for her daughter and has drawn no pension in nine years because the Regency has no record that her husband is dead. He does not say why. He cannot leave the Cut, cannot walk to the fort and back in a day with his chest, and cannot ask Vexa, because asking Vexa for anything is a split. He will ask the party, slowly, after the second thimble, and he will ask them not to say who sent it.

He also wants, with a quiet ferocity, for nobody to dig where the coins on the grave say.

### What he knows
The glass south of the road better than anyone but Vexa, and the Cut's pit to the inch. What the chest held: the silver, the seals, a roll of the regiment's muster with six hundred names on it that Vexa burned for kindling before he could stop her, and a slab of Lithic stone with writing on it that she kept. That the seals sit in the vault beside a box of half-burned imperial papers that Vexa's dowser reads at night with the door shut. That Jaspin Sweet has twice offered fifty silver a seal, which tells Ferrow the seals are worth more than fifty silver to someone. That the Concord's packer at the Cut writes down what the lottery pays out, and to whom.

### How he treats the PCs
As customers, slowly; as soldiers, with a pity he does not hide; as people who might be going to Whitepost, with great care. He likes anyone who reads the coin on the grave and puts it back. He likes a quartermaster's clerk, or anyone who talks like one, out of all proportion. He dislikes being hurried and anyone who touches the sash. [[Ilse Marrow]], if she ever came this far, he would recognise as a supply clerk at twenty paces and talk to for an hour.

### The secret
Old Tally was his father. Quartermaster-Sergeant Harl Gant of the 11th Foot, of Whitepost, whose widow Bethan, seventy-one, keeps the back room at the Sunburst and will tell you about her husband in the ash if you buy her the second cup, and about her son in the ash too, because she believes Ferrow died at Cinders with the regiment. He let her. A corporal with one lung who dug up the dead for a living was not, he decided in 3003, a son to bring home to a post-house, and every year since has made it harder to walk through the door. His sister Jenet keeps the oven and does not know either. The pensions seal is his way of sending his father home without going himself: with it in her hand Bethan is a widow on paper and can draw Harl's pension, and the Regency's Office of Pensions, which has not paid a garrison in two years, will at least have to write her name down. He has never said the word "mother" at the Cut. He will not say it to the party. A PC who has been to Whitepost and met Bethan, or who reads the name on the sash under his coat (GANT, H., Q.M.S., 11th), can work it out, and if they say it to his face he goes very still and asks them, slowly, not to tell her, and means it.

He leaves the coins. When he lifted his father out of the glass in 3008 he took the purse off the body before Vexa's count, sixty-two imperial silver, Tally's own wages, every coin dated, the earliest from the year the man enlisted. On the 30th of every month, the Unveiling, when Pallas is dark and the Cut is asleep, Ferrow takes the old coin off the grave, puts it in the purse, and lays the next one down, oldest first. "So he isn't paid last." He has thirty-eight left, which he has worked out is longer than his chest will hold. The diggers think the dates are a map, and three crews have gone bust digging where the dates told them to, and he lets them, because it keeps them off the grave and because, as he says when drunk, "a man who digs for a map deserves a map."

He also knows one thing he has told nobody: at dusk on 7 Ashfall, walking back from the fort with his chest bad by the glassland track where it passes a mile south of the Cut, he met a cart coming the other way, House-sealed, heavy, two drivers in grey, and in the morning Vexa told the Cut that nobody had seen a cart. He saw a cart. He will say so to the party in session 2 if they have been decent about the grave: it is the last witness on the road between the Burned Mill and Fort Mercy, and it puts the strongbox in Vexa's cellar without a fight.

**Skelt.** Skelt was the one Glasser in the Ashenreach who did not come to the grave at dawn on Tally's Day this year, and was not in the Smokes that night when every other Glasser was, and came in to the fort on the 5th with rope-burns on his palms and would not say where he had been. Ferrow keeps this for a drink and a name: ask about Skelt, by name, and he tells it.

### What changes
Carry the seal to Whitepost (or promise to, and mean it) and Ferrow is the party's friend among the Glassers for the campaign: honest charts, the cold ledge for a look at the hum, a word in Vexa's ear, and the whole truth about Skelt and the cart. Tell Bethan her son is alive and he hears of it by letter within the month, and does not speak to the party again, and walks to Whitepost that winter anyway, two days on one lung, and it is the best thing anyone does for him in the campaign and he will never say so. Rob the grave and he says nothing, and the Cut hears, and the party's water costs four coppers and comes slowly. Tell Vexa about the coins and he loses the grave; he does not forgive it. If he dies (he can, in a greywind, any session after 5: his chest), the coin on the grave stops changing, and the diggers notice before anyone notices him.
