### Look and voice
Fifty, small and square, with bread-coloured forearms and flour in the creases of her face like a map of every smile. She wears a man's apron, too big, folded at the waist and tied twice, and she does not explain it. Her hair is iron-grey and tied back under a cloth, and her eyes are the sort that watch a customer's hands and then their face and then their hands again. When she laughs she laughs completely, with her whole body, as a person laughs who has decided that is cheaper than the alternative.

She talks to the dough. She talks to the customers as if they were the dough. She is exact about prices and exact about grief, and keeps both on a slate.

> "That's tuppence for the dumpling and a penny for your dead mother, she'd have wanted you fed. Chalk or coin?"

> "Don't be sorry. Be hungry. I can use hungry."

### Who she is
Ada Pennock was a baker's wife on the old High Street, and is a baker's widow on the new one. She and her husband Tom kept the best bakery in the Cinder valley: Tom did the bread, a big, slow, shy man with hands like hams; Ada did the counter, the books and the talking. On the night of Cinders he took off his apron and went with the street to the old chapel of Hathe at the end of the High Street, because the priest had told everyone to come and keep the door. He was one of sixty who went. Forty of them, the Glassers say, are still there, sitting where they sat, under the river.

She came back in 3003 with a sack of flour, a trowel and her two girls, Nettie and Rose, who were then four and two, and are twelve and ten now. She built the oven first. She has been awake at the fourth night-bell every morning since, because, she says, she does not sleep so much as stand by.

She laughs at funerals. It is not callousness. It is a Raveler custom she took up in the first winter, from watching them do it, because she had no other way through.

### What she wants
For somebody to go down and bring up her husband's ring, which he took off to knead every morning of his life and put in the till-tin with the day's takings. She does not want the money. She wants the ring.

### What she knows
Every soul in the Ford and the Smokes by their bread. Who is short before they know it. That Vosk presses the crust like a man checking a wound and is no baker. That the Candle's brandy is a lie and Hennet knows it. That the Barrow lies beneath the river, and that the bakery's ovens are still standing in it, because every Glasser who has been down says so and Tom did not make bad ovens.

She also knows, because she has asked every Raveler and every Glasser who has been down, that her husband was one of the forty. She has asked each of them, in the same level voice: did he look at peace?

### How she treats the PCs
As customers first and as a project second. The first time she sees them she feeds them, and chalks it. By the second time she has asked them what they are doing on the hill, and by the third, whether any of them has been down. If a Raveler (Quorl) is among them she puts the best of the day's bread in front of him and leaves it there. She never asks outright. She says, "I don't suppose you're going down the culvert," and goes to see to the oven.

:::gm
**Secret.** She feeds the Ravelers free so that one of them will fetch the ring. She has not told a living soul this, and she does not know that her husband's apron went into the river in the flood of 3003 and was caught at the Sedgemere weir by [[Keld Owes-Nothing]], who keeps it folded on the shelf in her hut with the other things the river would not keep. If the party carries that news to her she will give them the Hearthday loaf, still warm, and cry in the oven-room with the door shut for as long as it takes.

Tom is in the fourth pew from the front, on the aisle, with his head turned a little toward the door. He still has the shape of his cap. If Quorl takes the rite there (see [[The Baker's Hoard]]) and asks for Tom by name, after the forty have said their sentence, Tom has something to say, and Quorl, who repeats any voice he has heard, can say it in Tom's voice to Ada.

She is not weak. If the Sleepless or Sallow's men ever come into her shop for a quarrel, she will stand between them and the oven with a peel in her hand, and nobody has tried it twice.
:::

== GM notes ==

Ada is the party's best "ordinary person who wants one thing" in session 1: the first moral weight before the Barrow, a reason to go in beyond the oil. She is cheerful, practical, and unafraid of the dead, because she has been waiting for hers for nine years. She does not cry on the table until the ring is in her hand. If the party brings it, give her one line: "He never wore it past the flour." Then let her bring them bread, and chalk nothing.
