When the ash settled, Cinderford was a hundred acres of broken stone around the stumps of a bridge. In the spring of 3003 AW the refugees came back to the few who had never left.

**Who.** Survivors, scavengers, the walking wounded of eight dead companies, and a ragged tide of refugees who had nowhere else to go. Reeve [[Odile Fairweather]], newly arrived and newly widowed, with two sons and a treaty-commissioners' charter, took the job of telling them where to put things. A five-arch bridge went up on the piers that had somehow survived. [[Halflings]] of the barge clans brought timber upriver and charged for it. [[Ravelers]] buried what they found, and charged more.

**How.** In grey. The only stone in reach was the rubble, and the only mortar was glass-dust from the nearby ruins, which dries the colour of ash and sets like iron. Every building in Cinderford is that grey, and strong, and a little sharp at the edges. The Regency sent a surveyor, who measured the rubble twice and went home.

**The Assessor's Office** was established in this period: one room, one clerk, a stamp and a promise that more were coming. Three clerks and a mountain of paper later, it is still one room.

**What it left.** A town of two thousand eight hundred that did not expect to stay. A habit of hanging a coat by the door. Grey glass-dust mortar worked into the creases of a child's hands, which no scrubbing shifts, and which the town's children show off as proof they were born here.

**How it is told now.** Residents tell it as a story of defiance, usually after the second drink. Visitors say they've never seen a place so determined to leave.

:::gm
Twelve of the first houses were built over cellars the builders did not dig. The glass-dust mortar rings faintly at red moon. The Smokes' oldest families know to keep their doors shut those nights.
:::

== GM notes ==

Use the rebuilding as the explanation for odd details in town: a wall that does not match its neighbour, a cellar no one admits to, a bridge that is older than the battle.
