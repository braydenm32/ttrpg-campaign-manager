A company that has not been paid in two years sings a great deal, because it is free. The Candle has one tune for everything, which Spoons says is the only tune Hennet allows, and the words change with the night. These are the ones everyone in the March knows at least a verse of.

### The Corporal's Song
Forty verses, nobody knows all of them, and anyone who claims to is making the last ten up on the spot, which is allowed; that is how it got to forty. Old Pike by the Candle fire knows every one, including the three that were cut, and will not sing them. Soldiers' doggerel, sung at [[The Last Candle]] to a tune that is "The Baker's Wife" slowed down, each verse ending in the chorus, which is the prayer of [[The Hanged Corporal]] with a tune on it. Vosk sings it flat. Marrow has never been heard to.

The chorus:

> *Corporal, I'm not doing it,*
> *I heard you the first time, sir.*
> *Corporal, I'm not doing it,*
> *and the rope's no worse than the war.*

The verses change by the night. Ten of them, in the order the Candle has been singing them this autumn:

> *They gave him a torch and a village to light,*
> *eleven old women and forty small fry;*
> *he looked at the thatch and he looked at the sky,*
> *and he put the torch down and he said:*

> *They hadn't a rope so they cut a tent-line,*
> *it broke the first time and it broke the second;*
> *the lieutenant said "third", and the Corporal reckoned*
> *the tree had more sense than the man, and he said:*

> *The Captain read out what the Regency sent:*
> *"You're paid," she said, "nothing," and nothing we spent;*
> *so here's to the wages that never were lent,*
> *and here's what the Corporal said:*

> *A man came up out of the ford on his own,*
> *a week in the water and cold to the bone;*
> *he'd not pay his tab and he'd not lie alone,*
> *so the Candle sang over him:*

> *The Assessor came riding, he came on a mule,*
> *with a form for the living, a form for the fool,*
> *a form for the dead and a form for the rule,*
> *and we signed the whole lot of them:*

> *The Sergeant's up red and he's writing you down,*
> *the dogs are all howling from here to the town,*
> *the dreamers are out and the lamp's burning brown,*
> *so roll over and tell him:*

> *The Saint sent a lady with eyes like a lamp,*
> *she'll heal up your fever and dry out your camp,*
> *she'll ask for your light and she'll ask for your stamp,*
> *and the kindest of answers is:*

> *Old men die in cots and they sit up to say*
> *the thing they'd have said if they'd said it that day;*
> *so take back the coin and we'll let the man stay,*
> *and we'll sing the long verse for him:*

> *The road to the Stone is a rope and a stair,*
> *the dead sing you down and the dead meet you there,*
> *the Warden's a statue, the statue's in prayer,*
> *and the prayer that he's saying is:*

> *The Grandmother's come in her chair to the gate*
> *with lawyers and ledgers and nine years of weight;*
> *she's bought all the debt and she's bought all the plate,*
> *but there's one thing she'll not buy, and that's:*

The **long verse** is the chorus with a line on the end, sung only at funerals, and the whole room comes in on it: *and neither was he.* The Ravelers consider it a hymn and have asked for it twice.

### The Eight Lanterns
A dirge, slow, one voice and a drum, for the companies whose lamps went dark on the wall at Cinders. Veterans sing it on the road east and on no other road, and the Ninth will sing it on the march in the last days, because there is nothing else to sing on that road. A verse for each company in the order they stood, and the refrain after every one. Four of the companies have names; the other four have verses that say so. The eighth verse has no words at all and is hummed, because nobody who knew the Eighth's name survived to put it in.

> *The First was the Salt Guard, they held the long wall,*
> *two hours they burned and then nothing at all.*
> *Eight lamps on the wall and they're dark, they're dark,*
> *and the ninth that nobody counted.*

> *The Second was Greywake's, the Spears and the shout,*
> *they sang as they stood and they sang as they went out.*

> *The Third had a name and the fire has it now;*
> *we knew it, we knew it, and none can say how.*

> *The Fourth was the Tithe, the Iron, the first,*
> *the first lamp to darken, and they say that's the worst.*

> *The Fifth, what was left of them, held to the last,*
> *and the last was a candle, and the candle burned fast.*

> *The Sixth was a company nobody can name;*
> *they'd a lamp like the others and it went out the same.*

> *The Seventh, the Ruddy, the Hundred, the cooks,*
> *who lit what they lit with no help from the books.*

> *(hummed)*

> *Eight lamps on the wall and they're dark, they're dark,*
> *and the ninth that nobody counted.*

[[Mother Hennet]] leaves the room when the Seventh's verse comes round, every time, and comes back for the humming.

### The Baker's Wife
Bawdy, old, from the pear country, and the tune everyone whistles when they are not thinking. The words are what you would expect from a song about a baker's wife and her oven, and they get worse. The rule is three verses in company. The fourth is sung only among men who have agreed in advance that none of them will repeat it. The fifth, the Glassers say, brings a greywind if it is sung on the Ash Road, and nobody whistles the tune east of the Treaty Stone, which is the only superstition the Glassers and the company share. There is said to be a sixth. Everyone who claims to have heard it is lying, and that is the lie everyone tells.

> *The baker's wife has a very fine oven,*
> *she keeps it as hot as the day she was wed;*
> *the baker goes out with his cart in the morning,*
> *the baker comes home and the oven's been fed.*

> *The baker's wife has a very fine rolling-pin,*
> *she rolls it out thin and she rolls it out wide;*
> *the miller came by for to borrow a measure*
> *and came out an hour after he went inside.*

> *The baker's wife has a very fine loaf,*
> *it rises, it rises, it rises again;*
> *and the baker says "wife, how's it rise so lovely?"*
> *and the baker's wife says "ask the men."*

The cookshop on Ford Street called [[The Baker's Wife]] is kept by a baker's actual widow, who named it with a straight face and has heard every joke.

### Ketterin's Luck
A Lithic song, very old, which humans sing wrong. The Lithic version is one word to a breath and takes an afternoon; it is about the mason who walked to the Ninth Stone and stood ([[The First Standing]]), and in it her luck is that she stayed. The human version is a drinking song, quick, and in it her luck is that she never had to get up again.

> *Ketterin's luck, oh, Ketterin's luck,*
> *she sat on the lip with a loaf and a cup,*
> *she drank the cup down and she looked at the Stone,*
> *and she's still sat there and she's still not alone,*
> *Ketterin's luck, Ketterin's luck,*
> *she never once had to get up.*

"Ketterin's luck" in talk means a small kindness that costs the giver everything, and the dwarves of the gatehouse will not sing the quick version and will not say why.

### The Ember-count
A Hollowborn children's rhyme from the Smokes, for counting out at games and for counting the twenty-three nights of the red moon, which the children do without being taught. It ends on the words the stone-touched dead say, and the children do not know where they got them.

> *One and two, the moon's new;*
> *three and four, there's the door;*
> *five and six, the latch, the latch;*
> *seven and eight, don't be late;*
> *nine and ten, say it again;*
> *eleven, twelve, the lamp's on the shelf;*
> *thirteen, fourteen, who's that knocking;*
> *fifteen, sixteen, who's that listening;*
> *seventeen, eighteen, pears are sweetening;*
> *nineteen, twenty, stand up gently;*
> *twenty-one, twenty-two, he's looking at you;*
> *twenty-three: not yet.*
> *Shut the door and count again.*

### The Stillwater note
Not a song. One note, low, hummed, held as long as the breath lasts: the sound of the bell under the lake at [[Stillwater]], which the drowned village's children brought back to the shore with them. Stillwater folk hum it when they want a conversation to stop, and it does. Mardle Ock at Fort Mercy hums three notes, not one, and his are not these.

:::gm
**The tenth verse.** The Lithic have a tomb-song for the Nine that counts ten and calls the last *the one who stayed soft* ([[The Stone Marriage]]). It is sung at a Standing, once, by whoever is left, and [[Warden Uthrem]] knows it and has nobody left to sing it for him. If a PC sings it at the Stone, slowly, he answers the tenth line, and what he says is the GM's to decide: that the tenth child is under the stair, or is the Sleeper's keeper, or is the reason he will not name the cold voice. Do not resolve it in this campaign.

**The fortieth verse** of the Corporal's Song exists and nobody sings it, because the words are "when the lantern goes out". Vosk knows it. At the Reseal ending, on the road home, he sings it once, flat, and the company comes in on the chorus, and that is the company standing down:

> *We held and we held and the holding is done,*
> *the lamp's gone to bed and the company's gone;*
> *so sing it the once and then sing it to none:*

**Songs as clocks.** The Eight Lanterns sung in the Candle is a Mutiny tick: it means the veterans think the Ninth is about to be the ninth verse. A soldier whistling The Baker's Wife east of the Stone has stopped caring, which is the same thing. The Ember-count is a free Sleeper Dreams prompt: a child counting it in the lane the day before a full is a scene for Tick. If a PC writes a verse of the Corporal's Song about something that happened at the table, it is canon in the Candle by the next session, and Spoons gives odds on whether it will reach forty.
:::

== GM notes ==

Sing badly and briefly. One verse at the table does the work of ten; the chorus is the thing, and the whole table should know it by session 3. The long verse at Pike's funeral in session 5 is the moment the song stops being a joke.
