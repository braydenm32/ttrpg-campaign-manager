name: Street Events in Cinderford
summary: Twenty small things that happen on Ford Street and round the square, marked for day or night, for when the party walks, waits or loiters.
tags: [ random, cinderford, ford-street ]
---

Roll when the party walks the length of Ford Street, waits in the square, or hangs about a shop door. One event is enough per walk. A *(day)* event wants market noise; a *(night)* one wants the lamps lit and the shutters up; *(either)* works in both. On Coinday the street is full and anything with a crowd gets a bigger one; on Wardday it is empty and quiet, and anything that happens in it is noticed from every window.

== GM notes ==

Use these as stage-business, not plot. Each one is a thirty-second scene and a thread: the party can ignore it, walk through it, or pull. If the party has a reason to be somewhere (a muster, a funeral, a deadline) let the event be an obstacle or an omen and keep them moving. Several hide a small truth for the observant: Hebbe's coin, Mote's window, the chalk eye, the clerks' step. Re-roll any entry that has happened to the party already this session, unless it is the goat.

Date gates: the mule (the seventh entry) is the Assessor's only from 15 Ashfall; Spoons's board (the eighth) is all about rent on the 15th of any month. If the date is 14 Ashfall (session 1), the second, ninth and eighteenth entries are the best choices: the bill, the window, the whistle.

== Entries ==

- (day) A goat loose on Ford Street, wearing a Bellwether bell, and a Smokes woman swearing at it in two languages. It is the Ninth's tithe goat. It is also Bellwether's goat. Hebbe has ruled it a beast of the Keep and, until it crosses the gate, a beast of the Tollivers, and wants 2 cp. The goat does not care, and neither will anyone who steps in it.
- (day) Sergeant Vosk at the gatehouse, reading the wanted bills aloud to the illiterate with commentary. Today: a woman wanted for a hen ("a big hen, a heavy hen, a hen with opinions"), a deserter of the Fifth, and a sum owing to Tollamund's house. He reads the sum last, slowly, and looks at the street. On 14 Ashfall and after he reads *Dannic Rook*, and does not comment.
- (day) Mistress Hebbe rings a coin on the gatehouse step, listens, and looks up at the street. She puts the coin in her pocket, not the toll-box, and goes on looking. Anyone who follows her gaze finds only the Reeve's hat. A coin that rings wrong is the Reeve's job this month.
- (either) Dunny Pike crossing the square at a run with a message he has been told to deliver and has not been told what it says. He will pass it to anyone for a pear. It is a note from Fennick Bottlebrook to the Baker's Wife, an apology in advance with a figure at the bottom, and he reads it out loud.
- (day) The wind turns, and the steam from the Salt Lake drifts over the street. For a minute a stretch of Ford Street smells of oranges and everyone slows, without deciding to, as if the street had remembered something warm.
- (night) A chalk eye, fresh, on a shutter: round, open, with a line under it. Nobody saw it done. The shopkeeper takes a wet cloth to it with no change of face and no change of pace, and does not meet the eye of anyone who is watching. By morning there is one on another door.
- (day) A mule refusing the hill. Before the 15th it is a courier's, with a Regency pouch and a Regency boy who has walked it the length of the street twice. From the 15th it is the Assessor's, and Pell Quillon is apologising to it, steadily, in a low voice, with one hand on its nose.
- (day) Spoons at a table in the street with a chalkboard, posting odds, and a crowd forming. Today: the Reeve's hat (a square-crown, three to one), whether it will rain (grey, even), and whether the Assessor survives the week (four to one, long; Spoons is being generous). On the 15th the board is all about rent, and everybody is short.
- (night) A light in the upstairs window above the cooper's, where the Assessor's room is. A thin figure in a scarf is standing in it, looking down at the street. He is not writing and he is not reading. When someone looks up he steps back, and the light goes out.
- (day) A Raveler cart, grey, with a grey pony and a cheerful whistle. The lane goes quiet as it passes; hats come off; a child counts the crows on the roof and gets a different number from her brother. The cart is full. The Raveler on it nods to the party as a colleague nods to a colleague.
- (either) Thistle Weirford on the gatehouse step, drumming a message to the Keep that nobody on the street can read. She is frowning. When she stops, a soldier at the Keep gate a quarter-mile away answers on a shield, and she nods and goes in.
- (day) A Glasser at the bridge-head leaving his pick on the step, as the rule is, and arguing with Hebbe whether the pick is a weapon or a tool of the trade. "It's the same thing in my hand, madam." "It is not the same thing in a Reeve's court."
- (day) Tollamund's three clerks walking three abreast down the middle of Ford Street, in step, grey and unhurried. Carts stop. A water-seller steps into a doorway. Nobody makes a decision about it; the street simply gets a little narrower while they pass.
- (day) A Hollowborn child stops dead in the road to let somebody pass who is not there. Everyone makes room. A soldier, who has seen it before, steps round the same empty place without being asked, and touches his cap to it.
- (day) A kiln-hand down in the road, sitting in the dust with his hands on his knees, breathing slowly through a face the colour of the paving. A crowd. A small, brisk woman in a pear-coloured apron arrives at a run with a bowl and a cloth and clears it by saying "Out" once.
- (day) The Reeve crossing the square in the hat of the day. Roll it: a tall felt with a fox's tail; a straw one with a wasp; a mariner's cocked hat; a widow's black with a bit of the sun; a baker's cap with flour on it. Half the street checks the odds. She does not look at the soldiers' book and everyone knows she has seen it.
- (either) A pilgrim asking the way to the Treaty Stone. Three people give three different directions. All three are right, depending on how long the pilgrim is willing to walk. The pilgrim thanks all of them, and takes the one that goes past a baker's.
- (day) Somebody whistling "The Baker's Wife" and somebody else telling him not to. It is Dree Ashfoot, who is a sapper and cannot be told. A wooden spoon sails out of the Baker's Wife's window and hits him on the back of the head. He whistles the second verse, with feeling, and leaves.
- (day) A courier from the capital asleep against the Bell post in the square with his pouch under his head. A boy is circling him. The boy is Nib, and he is not stealing the pouch; he is looking at the seal, which is not one the Assessor's Office uses. He sees you looking and walks away, fast, eating a biscuit.
- (night) The street shutting shop by shop from the west: the Bridle & Yoke, the Forge, the Turncoat, down to the square. The forge's hammer is the last to stop, and in the silence after it, for a moment, you can hear the river under the street.
