> A crossroads in low green country, where four lanes meet under one black oak, split and scorched down the north side and in full leaf down the south. From its lowest limb hang strips of rope, cords, bootlaces, halters, a hundred knotted rope-ends in grey and tan, and between them, pinned to the bark with every kind of nail, buttons: brass, horn, bone, regimental, hundreds of them, so that the tree seems to be covered in small dull coins. A bench. A dish of candle-ends. A boy of ten sweeping the road around the roots with a besom, with great seriousness. Behind him, a village of slate and new thatch, every roof exactly the same age.

### The lie of the land
No building in Dunmeer is older than fifteen years. The slate is the same grey, the thatch the same gold, the doorframes the same oak; whoever built the place built it in one season, in one temper, and everything shows it. The old village stood on the same crossroads and burned on 12 Frostcall 2996, and the new one was raised on its ash by its own people, who came back from three miles off in the spring and rebuilt without architects. It is a tidy village, a resolute one, and a very quiet one on every day but one.

The four lanes meet at the oak, and the oak is the village. It is the one tree that stood in 2996 and the one that survived, split by fire down its north face, and green on its south. The people of Dunmeer do not call it a gibbet. They call it **the Tree**, and say so with a mildness that ends the conversation.

The village keeps cattle, cheese, and the Tree. Three hundred souls: dairy-families, carters, a smith, two alehouses (the Rope and the Other), a chapel to Maelis, and a **cheese-shop**, [[Lennard's]], which was a cheesemaker's farm before the war. Their reeve is no one. They have a **Moot**, which meets under the Tree on the first Restday of each month, and its members are called, when they are called anything, **the Forty**. By the roll of 2996 the village held eleven old people and forty children. The eleven are mostly dead now. The forty are twenty-odd years old and run everything.

### How it is told here
Everywhere else the story ends *and the village burned anyway*, and soldiers like that part least. In Dunmeer it does not end there, and it is not told to visitors. A visitor is told, "He said no," and given a strand of rope.

### What is here
- **The Tree** and its shrine. A bench, a dish of candle-ends, a cup of salt, and the knotted rope and buttons. Visitors tie a strand and take a button; it is considered bad luck to take two.
- [[Lennard's]], the cheese-shop, on the east lane, kept by [[Mab Lennard]].
- **The Rope** and **the Other**, the two alehouses, run by two cousins who do not speak. The Rope sells ale; the Other sells ale and the Rope's complaints.
- **The Maelis chapel**, with a hearth, a kettle, and a wall of names cut in slate: the eleven and the forty, in one column, with the date.
- **The burn-ground**, a half-acre of grass behind the chapel where the old village stood, left unbuilt, with a single scorched doorstep.

### Who is here
[[Mab Lennard]] behind her counter. The Forty, in their twenties, doing everything, mildly suspicious. The Tree's sweeper (a different child each week: it is a punishment and an honour). Pilgrims, soldiers on leave, deserters who have prayed for a particular refusal. The prefect's tax-collector, once a quarter, who has been told not to bring rope.

### Rumours
- The oak has not budded on its burned side since 2996, and has never dropped a leaf on the other.
- Pray under the Tree and a rope will break within the week.
- The Regency has banned rope in Dunmeer twice, and Dunmeer replied with cord.
- Mab Lennard is the Corporal's sister, and she will not say his name.
- A half-burned levy roll in the shrine reads *H. Len-*, and above it a Salt Guard badge is nailed to the bark.

:::gm
**Which are true.** 1. True, and no gardener can explain it. 2. About one in five, by Mab's count, which is better than the Regency's. 3. True: once in 3003 (the prefect's own carriage-traces were cut that night, and the village produced a signed receipt for "cords") and once in 3008. 4. True. 5. True; the badge is Marrow's old company, the Salt Guard, and the roll is a scrap of the muster of the levy that served under it.
:::

### Hook
The half-burned levy roll in the shrine reads *H. Len-*; a Salt Guard badge is nailed above it. A soldier who has prayed here knows what the roll says. A soldier who has not will not look.

### Things to do
Tie a strand and take a button. Buy a cheese from [[Mab Lennard]] and be told, once, that the Corporal "gave it up." Sit under the Tree on Rope Day with the pilgrims (6 Frostcall) and watch the Forty serve a cup around, and nobody drink until it reaches the newest. Visit the burn-ground. Count the names on the chapel wall and find the single column. Ask the sweeper why the Tree has to be swept.

:::gm
**What Dunmeer knows.** The trial took an hour and the hanging took four, because the tent-line broke twice, and in those four hours eleven old people walked forty children out of the east gate in a line, past the Tree, past the soldiers and the corporal and the lieutenant, and nobody stopped them, because nobody was looking at anything but the rope. By the time the thatch was lit there was nobody in the village but the cattle, and the levies, afterwards, were said to have done it badly. The village burned anyway; the point is that it was empty. That is the whole of the Dunmeer story, and the Forty tell it only to someone who has refused something at cost.

**The Forty.** The forty children of 2996 are the adults of Dunmeer: thirty-one are still here, the rest dead, married away or gone for soldiers. They do not discuss it. Among those who left was **Dunstan Bray**, eleven, a tenant's boy from a Sallow farm two miles off, sent to his grandmother's house for the week; she was one of the eleven. He stood in the line past the Tree and saw the corporal put the torch down and say "No, sir." He has never told anyone where he is from. He took service with the Sallows at fourteen and followed the lieutenant to the Ninth; he hears the Corporal when nobody else does, and Sallow does not. If a PC who wears the rope tells Bray the Dunmeer story in S4, or says "Dask's rule" to him with real weight, or brings him a strand from Mab, it is the way to turn him. He will weep. See [[Dunstan Bray]].

**The rope.** The levy marched under the First Company, the Salt Guard, and the lieutenant needed a rope; the Salt Guard's supply clerk, a young woman called Ilse Marrow, issued tent-line from the worst coil in her stores, twice. It broke twice. The first time, she did it on purpose; the second, she did not need to. She did not know why she had done it, and has never learned what the four hours bought. The third rope was a halter taken from the Lennard byre, and Mab handed it over. The village remembers a clerk's white face and nothing else; nobody in Dunmeer knows she is the Ninth Lantern's captain. If Marrow ever stands under the Tree and is told what the line bought, it will be the most important thing anyone has said to her since Cinders. See [[Mab Lennard]] and [[Ilse Marrow]].

**The Tree.** It is not yet a god's. It is a place a god was hanged, and every refusal said under it feeds the Corporal a little, which the Forty know and treat the way a farmer treats a good ram.
:::

== GM notes ==

Use Dunmeer for the prologue (the PCs may have come through, or been pointed at it by a veteran), post-S8, and Rope Day (6 Frostcall, about four weeks after S8). It is also a good place to learn what the Corporal's cause is: not that he refused, but that his refusal bought time.

Keep the tone grave and rural and sly: a people who live on a legend and are mildly embarrassed by it. Everything is tidy. The mourning is done with cheese.
