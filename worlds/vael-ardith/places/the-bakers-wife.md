> The window is the first one lit on the street, and it is lit by an oven. A low shop front with a broad counter, a shelf of loaves like grey cobbles, a rack of small pies under a cloth, and behind it all, through an arch, the glow of a brick oven big enough to stand in. The smell hits like a hand: hot crust, scorched sugar, pear, and under it the dry mineral smell of ash. A woman with flour to the elbows is lifting a tray out of the oven with a wooden peel and does not turn round. "Counter's the counter," she says. "Take what you can pay for, and chalk what you can't."

There was a bakery on the old High Street before the battle, and it was the best one in the valley, kept by Tom Pennock, who was a big, shy, slow-talking baker, and his wife Ada, who did the counter and the talking. When the sorcery came, the bakery went under the river with forty houses. Tom did not come back. Ada did, in 3003, with a trowel, a sack of flour, and a determination to start again where she could see the hill. She set up the second shop on Ford Street, a month after the forge, because a town that has nails will want bread.

She named the shop for the song because the song, which every soldier in the March whistles when he is not thinking, is about a baker's wife; and because she had been one. She says the name with a perfectly straight face to anyone who looks at the sign. She has heard every joke about it that can be made, in four dialects, including two that were new to her. She knows the fourth verse, and the fifth. She is not particularly bothered. She has had a great deal of practice at laughing at things that would break another woman.

The bread is grey. The barley is ground with the chaff in, the water tastes of ash, and the loaf comes out the colour of the plain and keeps a week on the road. The barley comes up from Fallowden on the Bridle & Yoke's cart with the wheat she saves for Hearthday, and she does not discuss the price. It is the only honest loaf in the March, which Fennick Bottlebrook, who owes her nine days of them, says in a tone of bitter respect.

### Menu
- Ash-bread, 1 cp the loaf, 2 for a double. Day-old, half price.
- Stone-pear dumplings, 2 cp: suet and barley, boiled, the size and heft of the fruit they are named for and about as digestible. "Kettle says the barge was named first. Kettle is lying."
- Hearthday loaf, 2 cp: marked with Maelis's cross and plaited at the ends the way Tom did it, baked only on Hearthday, and gone by noon. (Mother Brisk's is free, and Ada says hers is better. She is not saying which.)
- Pallas pies, 3 cp, on the 15th only: mutton when there is mutton and goat when there is not, in a round white crust with the moon on the lid.
- Soup of the day, 2 cp, bread free. It is whatever is left.
- Hot Mercy, 1 cp a cup, in winter: a thimble of the pear brandy in hot honey-water, mostly water. "Don't call it brandy in front of Hennet."
- Bread and dripping, 1 cp. Bread and nothing, free to anyone who looks hungry and does not ask.

### Who is here
**Ada Pennock**, at the counter. Her two girls, Nettie and Rose, twelve and ten, who knead at the back and are not allowed in the shop. A queue at dawn: kiln-hands, spearmen, the Reeve's cook. [[Mirrel Vosk]] at first light on Hearthday, who presses a loaf's crust with his thumb the way a man checks a wound and says nothing, and has never once bought a loaf. Dree Ashfoot's whistle from the street, drawn off by Ada's ladle. A Raveler or two, always, at the back table, with bread they have not been charged for. [[Fennick Bottlebrook]], once a week, apologising to the slate.

### Rumours
- The bread is grey because she bakes it with the dead's ash.
- She gives bread free to any Raveler who asks, and it is because they know something about her husband.
- Her husband is in the Barrow and she has never been down.
- Fennick owes her nine days of bread and the company will starve before it pays.
- Vosk is no baker, whatever he says, and Ada knows it.

:::gm
**Which are true.** 1. False. It is barley with the chaff in and water that tastes of ash, like every loaf in the March. 2. True, both halves: see below. 3. True. 4. Half true: nine days of the followers' loaves is 63 silver on the slate, and the company will not starve for it, but Fennick will go grey. 5. True. She has watched him press a crust nine years and has never said so. He once asked her, with enormous dignity, for a cup of her starter, "for later". She gave it to him. It lives in a crock in the Keep's kitchen, and [[Brannoc Saltash]] feeds it every third day and does not ask why.
:::

### Things to do
Buy bread at dawn and eat it on the street. Hear Ada out about the Barrow, and what a body leaves behind in an oven. Ask her about the fifth verse and be told, flatly, that it is about a different baker. Settle Fennick's slate for him. Watch Dree whistle, and Ada do something about it. Take the Hearthday loaf, still warm, to someone who needs it.

:::gm
**Ada's secret** is the ring. Tom took it off every morning to knead, and put it in the till-tin with the day's takings, as every baker does, because a ring in the dough is a ring in a loaf. On the night of Cinders, he took off his apron and left the oven lit, and went to the old chapel of Hathe at the end of the High Street because the priest had told the whole street to come and keep the door. He never came back. The tin was in the bakery. The bakery is [[The Glass Barrow]]'s area 3, under the ford, where a lump of glass with 23 silver and 140 coppers fused into it holds his ring in the middle. Ada knows the ring is there because the tin always stood by the oven, and the oven is where the hoard was found. She has never been down. She has fed every Raveler in the March for three years in the hope that one will fetch it. The quest is [[The Baker's Hoard]].

Tom's apron went into the river in the spring flood of 3003 and came up at Keld Owes-Nothing's weir at Sedgemere; Keld's slate has the line, *A baker's apron, flour in the seams.* Ada does not know. If the party tells her, Kettle's cousins can bring it up the river in a parcel tied with eel-twine, and Ada will cry in the oven-room, not the shop.

Tom is one of the forty in the Glass Chapel's pews (area 4). If Quorl takes the rite there, Tom's last words, after the sentence all forty say, are for her. See [[The Baker's Hoard]].

The warm stone loaf in the bakery's oven (area 3) is the last loaf Tom baked: the Hearthday plait he left in the oven, fused as it stood. She will want to see it, and will ask nothing for it, and will put it on the counter under a cloth on the next Hearthday and sell nothing.
:::

== GM notes ==

Ada is the funniest unfunny person on the street: brisk, dry, not unhappy, and so careful with her grief that it turns up in the flour. She laughs at funerals (the Raveler way), at the Candle's Cinders night, and at the Reeve's hat. She does not laugh when Tom's name is said, and the table should hear the difference. Give her the line "He never wore it past the flour" when anyone asks about the ring. Use her in session 1 as the first piece of the Barrow they hear about before they go down it.
