> The goat-track comes over a rise of black glass and there is a village below you, whole. Roofs, a square, a well-head, a kiln with smoke coming out of it, a lane with washing on a line. And people in the square, a great many people, standing. You are a hundred paces off before you see that they are the colour of the glass, that the woman at the well has her bucket half-raised and has had it half-raised for nine years, that the children by the kiln are children of dark glass with their faces turned toward the road. And then a living girl with one grey eye steps out from between two of them, a slate under her arm, and says, "You're new. Don't touch anybody. Uncle Jeb's cutting. I'll take you."

Ambercote was a village of the pear country, two hundred and some souls, kilns and goats and a cider-press, far enough south of the road that the armies went past it. On the night of Cinders the sorcery came over the downs from the east and the village went to glass in a breath, standing. Not melted, as the Barrow was, not fused into sheets like the glasslands on the road: every house stands, every wall, every person, exactly as they were at the moment the sky caught, turned to dark glass to the roots of their hair. Two hundred and twelve of them. The woman at the well. A smith with a horseshoe in his tongs. A wedding party outside the cider-house, the bride's hand up to her hair. Children. The village is intact and it is a graveyard where everyone is still standing up.

Eleven people were below ground when it happened: in the kiln cellar, the cider-house cellar and the well-shaft. They came up in the morning into a village of glass, and some of them, after a while, stayed. They live in four houses in the lee of the square, three families and an old couple, and their children, born since, have a grey eye. They graze goats on the ash-grass that grows south of the village where the glass gives out, cut slag from the glass-fields for the kilns at Cinderford, make a hard white cheese, and work every day within arm's length of their own dead. Nobody at Cinderford has ever counted them. The Glassers do not dig here. Ask why and you will be told about the smith's wife.

### What is here
- **The square**, where a hundred and forty of the standing are: market day, the moment the sky caught. In the middle, where a market cross would be, a waist-high grey stone with tall thin marks cut into it, top to bottom, that the villagers always called the Old Post and that is a road-marker of [[Ul-Tarran]]: see [[A Lithic Road-Marker]].
- **The lanes**, where the rest stand: at doors, at a window, two men carrying a ladder, a boy on a wall. Washing on the line is glass too and does not move in the wind.
- **The cider-house**, roof intact, press intact, the wedding party outside. The living use its cellar for the cheese.
- **The kiln**, still fired, where the slag is cut and sorted. Jeb Marrowick's cutting-floor is beside it, and the woman standing nearest to it is his wife.
- **The well**, brackish but drinkable, with the woman and her bucket beside it. Nobody uses the far side.
- **The four living houses**, in the lee of the square, with doors that face away from it and a goat-pen between them.
- **Two men in the square who were never villagers.** They stand at the square's eastern edge, in company tabards under the glass, with the salt-badge of the First Company, the Salt Guard, on their breasts, and nobody in Ambercote knows their names.

### Who is here
[[Jeb Marrowick]], glass-cutter, headman. His sister Mardy, who keeps the cheese and the kiln. Her daughter [[Cass Marrowick]], eight, who keeps the roll and speaks for the standing. The Pellows, Abram and Sukey, cutters, and their two boys, Dace and Pim, Hollowborn both. Old Haskard and his wife, who were in the well-shaft, and their grandson Tobin, who was born in it the week after. Twenty goats. Kettle Brindlewake's cousins twice a month, with salt and iron. And the two hundred and twelve.

### Rumours (in Cinderford)
- There is a village out in the glass where people are still standing, fused where they stood, and at red moon they turn to watch you.
- The Glassers tried to cut one out once and every figure in the village turned its head.
- The living out there talk to the dead, and the dead answer.
- Gammer Teal at Stillwater has kin standing in that square.

:::gm
**Which are true.** 1. True; this is the one village of the Ashenreach rumour ("True of one village. The Glassers do not dig it."). 2. True. In 3009 a Fort Mercy crew came with picks for the smith's wife, who is wearing a necklace of imperial gold under the glass. Jeb Marrowick killed the crew-boss with a cutter's pick at the first stroke, and in the same moment every standing figure in the square turned its head toward the sound, and the crew ran. Vexa came herself a month later, looked for an hour, and forbade the village to every Glasser on pain of the glass. She and Jeb have a truce: nothing of hers comes here with a pick; nothing of his goes to Gutter Market but slag and cheese. 3. Half. The living talk to them. Only the Hollowborn hear anything, and only at the full Ember, and what they hear is two words. 4. True: Aud Marrowick, Jeb's wife, standing by the kiln, was a Teal of the pear country, Gammer Teal's grand-niece, before the Teals ever saw the lake.
:::

### Things to do
Walk the square in daylight and look the dead of Cinders in the face, which nowhere else in the Ashenreach lets you do. Buy cheese. Read the roll from Cass's slate and find a name you know (Quorl will want all two hundred and twelve for the Court's ledger, and Cass will dictate them, slowly, for a day). Sit in the lee of the square at red moon with the living and watch the standing turn, as one, toward the east. Ask Jeb about the two men in Salt Guard tabards and watch him decide whether to tell you. Lay a hand flat on the Old Post and feel it turn, very slightly, under your palm.

:::gm
### The truth
The standing are not the Barrow's dead and not the stone-touched that rise at Corvel Rise. They are what the Weeping Orchard's trees are: Lithic-touched, the ward's own substance caught in a mortal shape. The Ninth Road of Ul-Tarran runs under Ambercote's square (the Old Post marks it: a waystation stood here before the pear country had a name), and when the sorcery of Cinders came over the downs the ward under the village took the blow the way the shard took the Orchard, and the village went to the ward's stone instead of to fire. They are, in a sense the Lithic would recognise and nobody else would like, standing their watch. At the full Ember they turn their heads east toward the Ninth Stone, every one, and a Hollowborn in the square hears them, faint, in two hundred voices: "Not yet." Cass hears it every red moon. So, once, did Jeb.

They are tied to the ward, and the ward's fate is theirs. **Let it crack**: they walk, all two hundred and twelve, east, with the dead of Cinders, and the living of Ambercote walk after them because what else would they do. **Reseal**: on the night of 9 Leafrot, as the crack closes, they slump, every figure in the square sagging an inch where it stands as if a long breath had gone out of it, and in the morning the woman at the well has set her bucket down. It is the epilogue's last image. **Use it**: they stand, and stand, and at every red moon they turn a little further toward whoever holds the flame.

**The two Salt Guard men** were never villagers. They were deserters of the First Company who ran south from the walls on the night of Cinders and got as far as Ambercote's square before the sky caught, and their names are on no roll: Cass has them on her slate as *Salt Man* and *Other Salt Man*. [[Ilse Marrow]] was the Salt Guard's supply clerk and would know their faces. The Dreaming Widow has told her Sleepless that the standing of Ambercote "hold the door" and that the two soldiers hold it hardest, and a Sleepless crew of Glassers from Tally's Cut means to cut them out at the next red moon and carry them to the Rent: see [[The Standing Village]]. Jeb will kill them. The square will turn.

**Casting here** frosts the glass of the nearest standing figure (a minor ward-node, as the Barrow). A Hollowborn who sleeps in the square at red moon dreams the Reseal dream, the door closing, which is Cass's dream and nobody else's in the March. A Lithic, or anyone holding the Ward Shard, could speak with the standing at a sentence an hour, and the first thing they would say is their names.

== GM notes ==

Ambercote is where the party can look at what the war did with the lights on, and where the campaign's ending has a face: two hundred faces, and one girl with a slate. Run the daylight visit as quiet horror, the red-moon visit as something else: the living of Ambercote stand with their dead at the full Ember, in the square, holding hands with glass, and it is not a ritual, it is a family. Keep the Glassers' fear of the place in view; it is the only law here.

Stillwater is the only hamlet on the grey lakes; Ambercote is in the southern glass and has no lake. The dozen living are never counted anywhere: the Hollowborn of the March stay "a few hundred", and these are "a dozen at Ambercote whom nobody counted".
