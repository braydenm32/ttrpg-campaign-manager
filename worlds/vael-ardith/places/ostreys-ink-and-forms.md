> A narrow shop between a notary and a pie-stall, the window full of paper: cream, buff, the pale blue of the Treasury, the grey of the Marches, pinned in fans with their numbers inked beneath. Inside, pigeon-holes to the ceiling, each with a brass number, and a ladder on a rail. The smell is gall-ink and sealing wax, and under it the sour note of the river. A small neat man in a brown coat stands on the ladder with his back to you and says, before he turns round, "Which number?"

The Hall of Records runs [[The Regency of Ardith]] on forms and does not supply them. This is not malice; it is a line in a budget, cut in 2983 when the Council had other things to pay for, and never put back. So a citizen who must file a Declaration, a Return or a Request for Permission buys the blank from a stationer, and the stationer buys the plates' use from the Office of the Records at a fee, and the whole Regency pays a copper a sheet for the privilege of being governed. Ostrey's has held the Records Street licence for thirty years, first under Wim's father and now under Wim, and sells more forms than any shop in the capital, which is to say more than any shop in the world.

Wim Ostrey is forty, small, exact, and the nephew of Hallam Ostrey, the hired general whose Free Host of three claimants' contingents sat round [[Ardmere]] for two hundred and eleven days in 2994 ([[The Siege of Ardmere]]). He will tell you he is no relation, in the voice of a man who has said it four times a day for seventeen years. What became of the uncle, nobody in the family says; the Regency's report on the siege says the matter was "concluded satisfactorily", and Wim sells that report too, 4 cp, Form H-19, nineteen pages, and has read it more often than anyone alive.

### Stock
- Plain sheet, Hall weight, 1 cp. Treasury blue, 2 cp. Marches grey, 2 cp, "and you will want three, because the Marches lose one".
- Form 44-A, Declaration of Non-Receipt of Imperial Funds, 3 cp. "Popular this year."
- Form 7-R, Receipt of Remittance, 1 cp. Sold by the hundred to garrisons, who sign them for nothing and send them back.
- Form 11-C, Request for Additional Forms, 1 cp. He sells it with a straight face.
- Form 9-K, Declaration of Wages Owed But Not Payable, Countersigned: **out of stock since Reaping**. He will take your name.
- Gall-ink, Hall black, 4 cp the pot; Treasury blue-black, 8 cp; red for the Seal, by licence only.
- Sealing wax, 2 cp the stick; Regency red, 5 cp, and he asks what it is for.
- Pens, cut, 1 cp each or a dozen for 8. Pen-knives, 3 sp. A clerk's travelling case with inkpot on a chain, 2 gp, which is where [[Pell Quillon]] bought his.
- Forms for the dead: a Census of the Deceased, Form 30-D, 2 cp, a page with forty lines. The Ashenreach's assessors order these by the ream.

### Who is here
Clerks on their way up the hill, buying the day's blanks for their desks out of their own wages. Notaries' boys. Country people with a letter from a prefect and no idea what it means, whom Wim treats with a gentleness that would surprise his customers. An elderly Vesh from the Archive who buys one sheet a day and has for twenty years. And, in Reaping, a quiet clerk with a cold who bought every 9-K in the shop and every one Wim could get from the Office in the fortnight after, paid in new silver, and gave the name of an Under-Collector who, when Wim wrote to confirm the order, had been dead since 3006.

### Rumours
- Ostrey is the besieging general's bastard and the Quiet Office lets him live because he is useful.
- The Hall stopped printing 9-K because the Council does not want anyone declaring what it owes.
- You can buy a blank Regency seal here if you know the word.
- The order-book under the counter shows who in the capital buys which form, and the Quiet Office reads it monthly.

:::gm
**Which are true.** 1. Half: he is the general's nephew, legitimate, and the Quiet Office lets him live because he is harmless and his order-book is useful. 2. False: the Hall prints them; one buyer took them all. 3. False, and he will sell you the form for reporting the question. 4. True. The order-book is Wim's own, in his own hand, and an Examiner copies it on the first Coinday of each month for a fee Wim does not refuse. He knows what they are. He also keeps a second order-book, the honest one, in which the clerk with the cold is written down with the name he gave, the dead Under-Collector's, and the date, 17 Reaping, and the words *paid in new silver, this year's date, which nobody has*.
:::

### Things to do
Buy a 44-A for the company and discover that the clerk behind you is buying a 7-R for the same garrison. Ask for a 9-K and be told, with real regret, that there are none in the capital, and have a name taken. Ask Wim about his uncle and get a lecture on the word "relation". Ask him about the clerk with the cold, after buying something expensive, and be shown the honest order-book, briefly, upside down, with a thumb on the name. Order a Census of the Deceased and feel his eyes on you as he counts the lines.

:::gm
The Quiet Office cornered the capital's 9-K supply in Reaping for a dull reason and a sharp one. The dull reason is that a 9-K is the form the Ninth Lantern must file to dispute [[Pell Quillon]]'s tax notice ([[The Assessor's Lost Form]]), and an Office that wanted the garrison's default to look lawful preferred that nobody in Caer Ardith could file the same form on the company's behalf. The sharp reason is that [[Ansel Mote]] writes his reports to the capital in a cipher of column widths on the back of 9-K blanks, because a tax form in a tax clerk's bag is the one paper nobody opens; the Office needed a stock of blanks that could not be traced to a sale, and bought them all, under a dead man's name. Mote's own tenth crate holds a ream of them. The clerk with the cold who bought them was not Mote, who was already in Cinderford by the 17th; it was another of the Office's clerks, who use the cold as a sign the way other men use a ribbon.

Wim's honest order-book is evidence: a Quiet Office purchase in a dead official's name, dated, in new Regency silver. Shown to Pell it is a second thread to pull beside the Treasury ledger; shown to Edda Haverleigh it frightens her into producing her copy of the zero. Wim will not hand the book over. He will let it be read, once, by someone who has bought a travelling case.
:::

== GM notes ==

Wim is a straight man, not a clown: precise, a little sad, scrupulous about the difference between what he sells and what it means, and privately certain that the Regency would collapse in a week if his shop shut, which is true. The joke of the shop is one joke only, the Hall that does not supply its own forms, and it is funnier the straighter he plays it. His uncle is the ache under him: he has read Form H-19 forty times looking for the sentence that says what happened, and it is not there.
